## Text

When a doctor decides to <em>prescribe</em> a new <em>medicine</em>, the <em>appointment</em> should include more than a quick signature. Patients need to understand why the <em>prescription</em> is needed, how the <em>drug</em> works, and what changes to expect during the first few days.

Before <em>treatment</em> begins, the doctor should ask about any <em>allergy</em>, previous side effects, and <em>tolerance</em> to similar <em>medicines</em>. For example, an <em>antibiotic</em> may be effective against an <em>infection</em>, but it can cause problems if the patient has reacted badly to the same class of <em>medicine</em> before.

Some <em>treatments</em> are taken as <em>tablets</em>, while others require a nurse to <em>inject</em> the <em>medicine</em>. In that case, the patient may receive an <em>injection</em> or a <em>shot</em> at the <em>clinic</em> and then be observed briefly afterward.

During follow-up visits, the doctor may <em>monitor</em> <em>symptoms</em>, <em>blood pressure</em>, or test results. If the <em>medicine</em> is not working well, they may <em>adjust</em> the <em>dose</em>, change the schedule, or <em>refill</em> it only after checking that it is still safe.
