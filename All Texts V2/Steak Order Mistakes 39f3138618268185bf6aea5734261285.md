Last Friday, Daniel took his sister to a busy downtown bistro for her birthday. The <em>server</em> described the evening <em>special</em> as a <em>mouthwatering</em> steak with roasted garlic butter, so they both <em>ordered</em> it as their <em>entrée</em>.

Daniel asked for his steak <em>medium rare</em>, while his sister chose <em>medium</em> because she disliked anything too pink. When the <em>plates</em> arrived, his steak looked <em>rare</em> in the center, almost cool, and hers was <em>overcooked</em>, dry, and <em>chewy</em>.

They tried a few <em>bites</em>, but the meat tasted <em>flavorless</em> and unevenly cooked. Daniel felt embarrassed, yet he politely called the <em>server</em> over and asked if they could <em>send back</em> both steaks.

The <em>server</em> apologized and brought a <em>replacement</em> for each of them within fifteen minutes. This time, Daniel’s steak was warm and juicy, and his sister’s was perfectly <em>medium</em>, not <em>undercooked</em> or dry.

By the end of the meal, they were laughing about the mistake. His sister said the corrected <em>dish</em> was worth the wait, especially with the garlic butter.
