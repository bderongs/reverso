## Text

In many offices, humor is treated as a sign of confidence and quick thinking. A <em>sassy</em> reply in a meeting or a <em>cheeky</em> comment by the coffee machine can make colleagues seem lively, <em>approachable</em>, and <em>clever</em>.

But the line between funny and hurtful is thinner than many people admit. A <em>flippant</em> joke about someone’s mistake may sound harmless to the speaker, while a <em>sardonic</em> remark can embarrass the person who is already under pressure.

Some forms of humor are sharper still. <em>Wry</em> observations can be charming, but <em>mordant</em> jokes about personal problems, appearance, or background often feel <em>nasty</em>, especially when they are repeated in front of others.

Workplace trainers say the warning signs are clear: if a joke is <em>obnoxious</em>, <em>coarse</em>, <em>crude</em>, or relies on making one person the target, it is unlikely to build trust. Even a <em>tacky</em> comment meant to “lighten the mood” can damage relationships when respect is missing.
