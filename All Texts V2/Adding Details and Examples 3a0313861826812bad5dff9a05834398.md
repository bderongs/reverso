## Text

Strong presentations rarely depend on facts alone; they succeed when speakers help the audience see why the information matters. To do this, a presenter should <em>elaborate</em> on <em>key points</em>, <em>expand on</em> important data, and, when necessary, <em>rephrase</em> complex ideas in simpler language.

A useful method is to <em>break down</em> a large idea into smaller parts. <em>For example</em>, <em>instead</em> of saying that customer satisfaction improved, a speaker might <em>delve into</em> survey results, response times, and service quality; <em>in other words</em>, the claim becomes clearer through evidence.

<em>Furthermore</em>, examples make abstract points easier to remember. <em>For instance</em>, a presenter discussing teamwork could describe a project where clear roles improved performance; <em>in addition</em>, a short comparison with a less organized project can make the lesson more convincing.

<em>Moreover</em>, details should support the main message, not distract from it. <em>Besides</em>, audiences appreciate structure, so each example should connect smoothly to the next point and help listeners follow the speaker’s argument.
