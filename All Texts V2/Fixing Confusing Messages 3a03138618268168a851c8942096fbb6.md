## Text

Last month, I watched my colleague Maya rehearse a client presentation about a new scheduling app. Her first <em>slide</em> was full of technical details, and the main benefit did not <em>come across</em>, so the team asked her to <em>clarify</em> the purpose before the meeting.

Maya decided to <em>rephrase</em> the introduction and <em>make it crystal clear</em>: the app would save managers two hours a week. She also used warmer <em>inflection</em> when she spoke, because her earlier tone sounded too flat for such a useful idea.

During the live presentation, one client asked an <em>off-the-cuff</em> question about costs. Maya did not panic; she paused, <em>broke down</em> the pricing into three simple parts, and <em>spelled out</em> what was included in each package.

At the end, she gave a short <em>recap</em> to <em>make clear</em> the problem, the solution, and the next step. This time, everyone <em>got the message</em>, and her <em>key points</em> <em>got through</em> without confusion.
