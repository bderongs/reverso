## Text

In a job <em>interview</em>, employers want more than a list of tasks from your résumé. They want to understand your <em>core competency</em>, the main <em>strength</em> that makes you effective in the role.

Start by separating each <em>hard skill</em>, such as data analysis or budgeting, from each <em>soft skill</em>, such as teamwork or adaptability. If the job is technical, mention that you are <em>tech-savvy</em> and explain any <em>specialized</em> tools you have used.

Use short examples to prove your <em>ability</em>, not just describe it. For instance, you might say, “I used <em>critical thinking</em> to identify a reporting error and created a faster process,” which sounds stronger than “I am good at solving problems.”

Employers also listen for <em>interpersonal skills</em> and <em>leadership skills</em>, even in non-management roles. A <em>hands-on</em> example, such as <em>training</em> a new colleague or calming a difficult client, shows that you are <em>skilled</em> in real workplace situations.

Finally, connect your <em>aptitude</em> to the company’s needs. This helps the interviewer see how your <em>strengths</em> can bring <em>value</em> from the first day.
