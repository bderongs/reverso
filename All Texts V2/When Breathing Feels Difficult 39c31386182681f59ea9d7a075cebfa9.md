## Text

Feeling <em>breathless</em> after climbing stairs can be normal, but sudden or <em>severe</em> <em>breathing</em> problems should never be ignored. Doctors say <em>chest tightness</em>, noisy <em>breathing</em>, or becoming <em>short of breath</em> while resting may point to asthma, <em>infection</em>, or a heart-related <em>condition</em>.

If a patient is panting, struggling to speak, or trying to <em>gasp</em> for air, the situation may be <em>urgent</em>. An <em>inhaler</em> can help people who have been diagnosed with asthma, but it should not replace medical advice when <em>symptoms</em> are new, worsening, or unusual.

Call an <em>emergency</em> service if someone’s lips turn blue, they seem confused, or they feel as if they might <em>suffocate</em>. A <em>choke</em> caused by food or another object is also <em>critical</em> and requires <em>immediate</em> action.

At the doctor’s office, be ready to describe when the <em>symptoms</em> began, what makes them better or worse, and any <em>medicines</em> you use. Clear details help clinicians decide whether you need tests, <em>treatment</em>, or rapid care.
