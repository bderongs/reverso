## Text

Across many urban <em>areas</em>, rising temperatures are changing daily life and public planning. In one coastal <em>region</em>, officials have begun mapping neighborhoods where older residents, outdoor workers, and low-income families face the greatest <em>impact</em> from extreme heat.

<em>Contemporary</em> city design now treats shade, water, and ventilation as <em>essential</em> services, not luxuries. Planners are adding trees, cool roofs, and reflective pavements to improve the local <em>environment</em> and <em>mitigate</em> dangerous temperature increases.

The public <em>sector</em> often leads these efforts, but businesses and community groups also play a <em>major</em> <em>role</em>. For example, supermarkets may open cooling spaces during heat waves, while volunteers check on residents who live alone.

Still, every city faces a <em>constraint</em>, such as limited budgets, old infrastructure, or a shortage of trained staff. Leaders must decide which projects are <em>feasible</em> now and which <em>require</em> long-term investment.

Successful <em>implementation</em> depends on clear <em>data</em>, public trust, and cooperation across departments. As climate risks grow, cities that <em>adapt</em> early can protect health, reduce inequality, and <em>create</em> more comfortable places to live.
