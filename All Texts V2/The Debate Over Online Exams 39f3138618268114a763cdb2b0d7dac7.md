## Text

Universities are reconsidering online exams after several semesters of mixed results. The policy remains <em>controversial</em> because it promises flexibility but raises questions about fairness, security, and academic standards.

Supporters present a <em>persuasive</em> <em>argument</em> that remote testing is <em>beneficial</em> for students who commute, work part-time, or live with health concerns. They also cite <em>credible</em> research suggesting that well-designed digital exams can measure learning as effectively as traditional tests.

Critics, however, say this <em>assumption</em> is <em>debatable</em> and may <em>contradict</em> real classroom experience. They argue that weak internet access, unclear instructions, and surveillance software can be <em>detrimental</em> to student performance and privacy.

The most <em>contentious</em> <em>issue</em> is cheating, yet some educators believe strict monitoring is only <em>justifiable</em> when it is transparent and limited. A <em>compelling</em> compromise may <em>involve</em> open-book exams, shorter response windows, and questions that <em>require</em> analysis rather than memorization.
