## Text

During last month’s power outage in our neighborhood, many residents discovered how quickly ordinary worry can turn into <em>fear</em>. Parents were <em>concerned</em> about children stuck in elevators, while elderly neighbors felt <em>afraid</em> to move through dark stairwells without help.

At the corner shop, a loud alarm made everyone <em>jumpy</em>, and one customer admitted she was <em>scared</em> it might signal a fire. Another man, <em>frightened</em> by the sudden blackout, began to <em>shake like a leaf</em> as he searched for his phone <em>in a frenzy</em>.

Emergency workers later said that <em>dread</em> and <em>trepidation</em> often spread faster than real danger during unexpected events. People may become <em>frantic</em> or even <em>terrified</em> when they lack clear information, especially if they hear rumors from <em>desperate</em> voices around them.

The city council now plans to improve public alerts and provide simple safety guides for residents. Officials hope that better communication will reduce panic and help people respond calmly when uncertainty takes over.
