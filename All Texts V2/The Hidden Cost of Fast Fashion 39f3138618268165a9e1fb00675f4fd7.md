## Text

In many cities, shoppers <em>consume</em> clothing at a speed that would have seemed impossible a generation ago. Cheap shirts and dresses appear online every week, <em>creating</em> <em>constant</em> pressure to buy more and wear <em>items</em> only a few times.

Behind these low prices is a global system of <em>labour</em> that often remains invisible to consumers. In some factories, workers face long hours, low wages, and unsafe conditions, while <em>corporate</em> brands compete to deliver new styles faster than ever.

The environmental <em>impact</em> is also <em>considerable</em>. Producing textiles <em>requires</em> large amounts of water and energy, and chemical dyes can have a <em>detrimental</em> effect on rivers and soil near manufacturing centres.

One important <em>factor</em> is the lack of strong rules in parts of the supply chain. Governments and international organisations have tried to <em>regulate</em> waste and workplace standards, but enforcement can be weak.

Experts warn that the <em>adverse</em> effects of fast fashion will grow unless consumers, companies, and policymakers change their behaviour. Buying fewer, better-quality clothes may be a small step, but it can help reduce demand for a system built on speed and disposability.
