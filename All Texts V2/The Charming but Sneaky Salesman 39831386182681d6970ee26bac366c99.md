## Text

When I bought my first car, the salesman seemed wonderfully <em>suave</em>. He was <em>witty</em>, <em>clever</em>, and so <em>savvy</em> about engines that I trusted him within minutes.

He praised my “excellent taste” and offered coffee while explaining the deal. Looking back, I realize he was also <em>shrewd</em> and <em>crafty</em>, steering me away from questions about the vehicle’s accident history.

His smile never slipped, but his methods were <em>sneaky</em>. He used <em>sly</em> comments about another interested buyer to pressure me, and he made the contract sound simple while hiding extra fees in the fine print.

A week later, my mechanic found serious problems. The dealership’s manager admitted the salesman had a <em>devious</em> habit of bending the truth, and some customers even called him <em>crooked</em>.

I learned that charm can cover a <em>greedy</em>, <em>materialistic</em> character. Now I listen less to polished compliments and more to facts, documents, and my own doubts.
