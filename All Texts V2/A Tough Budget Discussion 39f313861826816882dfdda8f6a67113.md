## Text

At Tuesday’s quarterly planning meeting, department heads faced a <em>thorny issue</em>: how to reduce spending without delaying key projects. The finance director opened with <em>tentative</em> figures, then invited managers to <em>voice their concerns</em> before any <em>decision</em> was made.

The <em>crux of the matter</em> was whether the marketing budget should be cut to protect product development. Several team leads wanted to <em>raise an issue</em> about hidden costs, while others asked for more time to <em>iron out</em> the details with their staff.

When one manager claimed the cuts would be painless, a colleague <em>called out</em> the assumption and asked for evidence. The chair encouraged everyone to <em>have a say</em>, but also reminded the <em>group</em> to <em>read the room</em> and keep comments brief.

By the end, employees felt they had been able to <em>speak their minds</em>, even if not every suggestion was accepted. Senior leadership may still <em>overrule</em> the <em>proposal</em> next week, but the discussion helped <em>clarify</em> <em>priorities</em> and build trust.
