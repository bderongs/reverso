## Text

In many households, conflict begins with a small <em>misunderstanding</em> and grows into a long <em>feud</em>. A <em>close</em> <em>relation</em> may feel ignored, while another family member believes that <em>loyalty</em> means staying silent, even when something unfair has happened.

Counsellors say that <em>related</em> problems often become harder to solve when people accuse each other of <em>hypocrisy</em>. A <em>faithful</em> sister or a <em>devoted</em> father can still act defensively, and a <em>vindictive</em> comment made in anger may deepen <em>antipathy</em> for years.

Yet families sometimes change when one person shows <em>mercy</em> instead of pride. <em>Sympathy</em> for another person’s <em>disappointment</em> can offer real <em>consolation</em>, especially after a painful argument.

Experts suggest that relatives should speak honestly, <em>listen</em> without interrupting, and avoid bringing up old mistakes. If both sides accept responsibility, they may finally <em>make up</em> and rebuild <em>trust</em> slowly.
