## Text

Maya had spent three months negotiating a software partnership with a national retailer. At first, both sides were excited, but by the final meeting, the retailer seemed <em>on the fence</em> about the pricing.

Her director warned her that the project had reached a <em>fork in the road</em>. If they offered another discount, they might pass the <em>point of no return</em> and damage future margins.

Maya wanted to <em>keep options open</em>, but she also didn’t want her team to <em>second-guess</em> every proposal they had made. Nothing was <em>set in stone</em> yet, so she asked for one clear list of concerns.

The retailer replied with new demands, including free support for two years. Maya felt they were trying to <em>move the goalposts</em>, and the support request became the <em>deal-breaker</em>.

The next morning, she decided to <em>draw a line in the sand</em>. She politely explained that without the original terms, the agreement was <em>dead in the water</em>, and both teams would be <em>back to square one</em>.
