## Text

When I arrived in Toronto for my first semester, I believed my English was strong enough for daily life. In the classroom, however, I quickly realized that academic language depended on <em>context</em>, and a familiar word could carry a <em>distinct</em> meaning in a lecture or article.

My first research seminar changed the way I <em>perceived</em> learning. Each <em>individual</em> student was expected to <em>interact</em> with professors, question <em>sources</em>, and explain how evidence could influence an <em>argument</em>. I had to <em>acquire</em> new vocabulary not by memorizing lists, but by noticing how terms were used in real discussions.

Outside class, I learned to <em>adapt</em> to another <em>aspect</em> of university culture: group work. At first, I stayed quiet because I worried about mistakes, but my classmates encouraged me to share ideas. By the end of the year, I was not only more confident in English but also more aware of how language shapes thought.
