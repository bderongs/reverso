## Text

During last month’s budget meeting, Maya presented a plan for replacing the company’s outdated customer database. She began by <em>making a case</em> for faster service, fewer errors, and better reporting.

The first <em>objection</em> came from the finance director, who worried about the cost. Maya <em>acknowledged</em> the <em>concern</em> and <em>conceded</em> that the first year would be expensive; <em>nevertheless</em>, she showed how support calls would drop within six months.

Then a senior manager offered stronger <em>pushback</em>, saying the staff might resist another software change. <em>Instead</em> of trying to <em>sidestep</em> the <em>challenge</em>, Maya shared <em>feedback</em> from team leaders and <em>redirected</em> the discussion toward training and practical support.

When someone claimed the old system had never caused delays, Maya politely <em>set the record straight</em> with three recent examples. She did not <em>press the point</em> too aggressively, but she used clear evidence to <em>win over</em> several doubtful colleagues.
