## Text

In universities and workplaces, people face a <em>constant</em> flow of claims from articles, reports, and social media. A <em>rational</em> reader does not accept every statement immediately but asks whether the <em>source</em> is <em>credible</em> and the evidence is <em>valid</em>.

Critical thinking helps us <em>scrutinize</em> information before forming an opinion. We may <em>deduce</em> a conclusion from clear facts, but we should also <em>infer</em> what is only suggested, while recognizing any <em>assumption</em> that shapes our judgment.

This skill is especially important when <em>arguments</em> contain a <em>fallacy</em> or a <em>contradiction</em>. For example, a speaker may offer an impressive <em>conjecture</em> about climate policy, yet fail to <em>distinguish</em> between <em>data</em> and personal belief.

Students preparing for academic study need this habit because professors expect independent evaluation, not simple memorization. By questioning evidence and comparing perspectives, learners build stronger <em>arguments</em> and make decisions that are both careful and informed.
