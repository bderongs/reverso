## Text

Last semester, I joined a small environmental science project at my college. Our team decided to <em>conduct</em> a <em>survey</em> to <em>investigate</em> why students rarely used the recycling bins placed around campus.

At first, the results seemed ordinary, but one pattern was <em>remarkable</em>: many students did not know which materials were accepted. This was a <em>noteworthy</em> finding because the bins had clear labels, yet the information was still not reaching people.

During the presentation, I used graphs to <em>demonstrate</em> the connection between confusing signs and low recycling rates. I also tried to <em>highlight</em> <em>comments</em> from students, especially one <em>insightful</em> suggestion about using pictures instead of long instructions.

When my professor asked how we would improve the study, I took a breath and <em>responded</em> honestly. I said we needed a larger sample before we could <em>conclude</em> that better signs would change behavior across the whole campus.
